# Research Brief — Tracing Public Funding to Addiction and Indigenous Health Services: A Private Farm Research Pilot

## Executive summary

A 16-record pilot tested whether public funding announcements and disclosures could be traced to recipients, services and later operational evidence across Miller and Miller North. Four records reached service-level funding confirmation, while broad budgets and health-authority totals generally stopped at program or organization level.

## Scope

Ten British Columbia/federal addictions funding records and six Indigenous-health accountability or governance funding records were selected from existing private chains. Official budgets, announcements, grant disclosures, health-authority reporting, Indigenous-organization reporting and implementation sources were preferred.

## Key findings

- Named grants and contribution agreements produced the cleanest recipient-to-service traces.
- Road to Recovery has strong implementation evidence but no authoritative site-level allocation of the $23.7-million annual or nearly $154-million expansion envelopes.
- FNHO demonstrates why governance authority, funding source and operational control must be represented separately.
- A funded curriculum announcement and an operational Alberta advocate role remain incomplete financial traces for different reasons.
- Audited health-authority statements are valuable context but rarely prove service-level spending.

## Evidence highlights

- B.C. announced $4.39 million over three years for New Roads and later sources confirm the operating program.
- Health Canada reports $6,065,479 for Kílala Lelum's prescribed-alternatives and Elder-led healing project.
- ISC announced $1.17 million to establish FNHO; a later federal grant disclosure identifies a separate $775,000 agreement.
- B.C. officially connects its $171.8-million Indigenous Fund to In Plain Sight recommendation 17 at program level.

## Policy/service relationships

- Road to Recovery commitment → program funding → health-authority implementation → operating service, with no site-level amount.
- SUAP contribution → named recipient/project → described service delivery.
- First Nations governance resolution → federal establishment funding → operating ombudsperson office.
- In Plain Sight recommendation 17 → provincial Indigenous Fund → Indigenous-led service program, with recipient allocations unresolved.

## Implementation status

Five records reach service-level or network operational-result confirmation, six confirm funding only at program or recipient level, three carry explicit non-attribution boundaries, one funded output remains unverified, and one operating role lacks a public role-specific amount. These statuses do not evaluate adequacy or effectiveness.

## Unresolved questions

- Can a CMHA BC grant document map a specific bed allocation to a named Miller resource?
- Was the St. Paul's cultural-safety curriculum completed and deployed in all three named platforms?
- Can a current procurement or contract record establish the value of a selected contracted service without relying on supplier payments alone?
- Which later audits report at the same program scope as the selected funding records?

## Method

Existing private policy and accountability chains were screened for public-money relevance. Amount, recipient, purpose, scope and recurrence were recorded only when stated. Every relationship required an authoritative source; funding, operation, utilization and outcomes remained separate stages. No overlapping amounts were aggregated.

## Sources

- B.C. budgets, estimates, news releases and ministry annual reports
- Fraser, Vancouver Coastal, Island and Provincial Health Services Authority reporting
- Health Canada funded-project registers and contribution announcements
- Indigenous Services Canada funding announcements and Open Government grant disclosures
- FNHA audited, annual and evaluation reporting; FNHO governance and annual reports

## Limitations

The sample is purposive and private. Announcement values may be maximums or envelopes rather than expenditures. Audited entity statements generally lack service detail; supplier lists lack purpose; operational evidence does not establish effectiveness; and no value-for-money, wrongdoing or adequacy conclusion was attempted.
