# Miller addictions policy, law, and government benchmark

Date: 2026-09-05
Status: private research benchmark; not imported or published

## Result

The benchmark contains 73 distinct candidates grouped into 21 policy chains. Sixty records are B.C.-jurisdiction records and 13 are directly relevant federal records. Every candidate has at least one official primary source. Six instrument-level records require owner review. The dataset also tracks 30 specific recommendations or commitments and 15 candidate relationships to real-world Miller services.

The strongest architectural finding is that Miller can reuse the Miller North temporal pattern without sharing subject data:

`system condition → recommendation → response → formal rule or commitment → implementation evidence → service availability → later evidence`

Policy activity, implementation, service availability, utilization and outcomes remain separate claims.

## Method

Research began with B.C. Coroners Service and Provincial Health Officer reports, then followed named recommendations forward into ministry responses, policies, professional standards, health-authority service evidence, audits and evaluations. Federal legislation, exemptions and judgments were included only where they directly govern B.C. addiction care or harm-reduction services.

The search used 10 web-discovery calls and 40 targeted queries. Sixty-eight authoritative source records and 13 local architecture/fixture units were inspected. No Tavily, local inference model or additional cloud research model was invoked; measurable external cost was $0.

## Research-screen findings

### Addiction treatment policy

The highest-yield records were the Adult Substance Use System of Care Framework, the 2023 opioid-use-disorder guideline, pharmacy and nursing standards, Road to Recovery documents, and supportive-recovery legislation and standards. The formal owner of a rule varies: legislation and regulation set facility requirements; regulators set professional conditions; BCCSU supplies clinical guidance; ministries fund and direct programs; health authorities establish operational service pathways.

### Toxic-drug and overdose policy

The 2016 emergency declaration, Ministerial Order 488, naloxone regulation, OPS minimum standards, prescribed-alternatives policies and the decriminalization exemption form several defensible change histories. Later service and utilization counts demonstrate that some interventions were operational. They do not prove population-level effectiveness.

### Coroner and public-health material

The 2018 and 2022 panels are unusually productive because they name responsible systems and repeat themes: treatment oversight, OAT/iOAT access, drug checking, safer alternatives, time-bound planning and a continuum of care. The 2023 panel provides a clear repeated-recommendation signal. The 2024 Auditor General report is the strongest independent official implementation check because it documents both operational programs and unresolved barriers/reporting gaps.

### Law and regulation

The benchmark preserves separate categories for statute, regulation, ministerial order, federal exemption and court judgment. Binding status is explicit. The Controlled Drugs and Substances Act, PHS judgment, section 56/56.1 exemptions, Good Samaritan Drug Overdose Act, B.C. Public Health Act, Mental Health Act, Community Care and Assisted Living Act and related regulations are not flattened into “policy.”

### Professional standards

Professional standards yielded precise effective dates and supersession information. Examples include PPP-66, the 2026 controlled-drug delivery standards, nurse-practitioner prescribing standards, certified-RN OUD standards and CPSBC training expectations. Clinical guidelines are stored as non-binding unless an authoritative regulator separately makes adherence a condition.

### Government commitments and services

Government announcements became strong implementation evidence only when a later authoritative source identified an operating service, date, location or measurable capacity. Creekside’s ten added beds, the Hope RAAC satellite, Access Central, the OAT Access Line, VCH’s 18-bed relocation, four Maple Ridge youth beds, Inlet Recovery Home, and the twelve First Nations-led beds meet that threshold. Terrace remains “implementation underway” because property acquisition and planning are evidenced but the promised capacity was not yet reported open.

## Claim-safety decisions

- Announcements are not treated as implementation.
- A strategy is not treated as proof that its services are operational.
- Aggregate provincial bed totals are not automatically reconciled to earlier announcements because fiscal announcements can overlap.
- Later mortality or another incident is a recurrence signal, not proof that a reform was ineffective.
- The Mental Health Act is linked only as governing context; the benchmark does not assert that substance use alone meets statutory criteria.
- No causal-effectiveness conclusion is made for OPS/SCS, prescribed alternatives, decriminalization, OAT or treatment expansion.
- No individual patient or deceased-person information was collected.

## Gaps

The most important next evidence gaps are recommendation-specific completion reports for the 2022 30/60/90-day action-plan request, full reconciliation of the 2023 and 2024 bed commitments, provincewide verification of every Road to Recovery access line, implementation reporting responsive to the 2024 audit, and current legal-version review of forthcoming federal controlled-substances regulations.
